You're at your kid's soccer game, half watching, when your phone buzzes. It's the shop. A regular customer wants a rush order and nobody there can tell her yes or no without you. You step away from the sideline, make the call in ninety seconds, and walk back like nothing happened. Except it happens again next Saturday. And the one after that.

At some point you decide the fix is a new hire. More hands, fewer calls to you, right?

## The part a new hire can't absorb

Maybe. But before you post the job, ask a harder question: is this a capacity problem, or is it a "nobody else knows how I make this call" problem? They look the same from the outside. They need completely different fixes.

A capacity problem means too much work and not enough people to do it. Hire somebody, train them on the existing process, and the extra hands solve it. A knowledge problem is different. The decision follows a rule you've never written down, so there's nothing on paper for a new hire to learn from. Hand them the job and they can't make the call either. They just have to ask you, same as before, except now you're training someone to come find you.

Say you hire someone to take the rush-order calls. The new person picks up the phone, then texts you: should I say yes to this one? You're still the bottleneck. You just added payroll.

## A fast way to tell which one you have

Ask yourself one thing: if you handed this job to a brand new hire today, with nothing beyond "here's how we usually do it," could they make most of the calls themselves inside a month? If yes, it's a capacity problem. Go hire. If the honest answer is "no, they'd have to ask me every time," you have a knowledge problem first, and that needs fixing before the job post goes up. Otherwise you're paying someone to ask you questions instead of paying no one to ask you questions.

## Why this gets missed

Owners usually notice they're busy before they notice why. Busy feels like a people problem, so hiring feels like the obvious answer. But a decision nobody has written down isn't a staffing shortage. It's a documentation gap wearing a staffing costume. Hiring someone smart to stand next to that gap doesn't close it. It just gives the gap a second person to confuse.

That rush-order call is a good example because the rule you use is probably simple. Something like: yes, if it's a regular customer and the stock is there; no, if it would bump another promised order. Written down, a new hire can make that call on day one. Left unwritten, they're stuck asking forever, and the new hire's real job quietly becomes "relay questions to the owner."

## The lesson

A hire fixes a capacity problem. It does not fix a problem nobody else knows how to solve, because you never wrote down how you solve it. Before you decide you need a person, decide whether you actually need a decision in writing.

<Takeaway title="One move to make this week">

1. Starting today, jot down every question someone brings you, for one week.
2. Sort each one: could anyone with the steps answer it, or does it need your call? If the "needs me" pile is bigger, hold off on the job post.
3. Take the most frequent "needs me" question and write the yes/no rule you actually use for it, including the exceptions.

</Takeaway>

The ninety seconds on the sideline were never the real cost. The rule nobody else could see was.
