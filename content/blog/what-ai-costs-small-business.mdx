Picture the moment. You're scrolling between customer calls and someone pitches "AI transformation," price tag with five zeros in it. You close the tab, go back to writing tomorrow's social post by hand, and file "AI" under things for bigger companies with bigger budgets.

That reaction makes sense. Most AI marketing is aimed at enterprise buyers with a procurement department and a software budget line. But that's not what AI costs a business your size. Believing it is costs you something real: hours a week spent on work a free tool could do in minutes.

There are two fears wrapped up in that reaction, cost and complexity. Neither one holds up once you look at what small business owners actually use.

## Myth one: it's expensive

Here's the honest breakdown, no pitch attached.

The general-purpose AI tools most owners actually reach for, ChatGPT and Gemini, both have a free version. You don't need a credit card to open one and ask it to draft a follow-up email, summarize a long document, or write five versions of a social post so you can pick the best one.

ChatGPT's paid tier has held at $20 a month since it launched in 2023. That's the paid step up, not the entry price. The entry price is free.

If you outgrow the free tier, most paid steps up land somewhere between $5 and $20 a month per person, closer to a streaming subscription than a software contract. No IT department required. No six-week rollout. No contract to sign. You turn it on, you use it, you cancel it if it's not worth it.

The real cost isn't the subscription. It's the time you spend picking which one task to hand over first, and the discipline to actually try it instead of adding "look into AI" to a list that never gets shorter.

## Myth two: it's complicated

The other fear is that using AI means learning new software, or hiring someone who already knows how. Also not true for the version that helps a business your size.

If you can type a text message, you can use a free AI chat tool. You open it in a browser, same as email. You type what you want in plain English. It writes back in plain English. There's no dashboard to configure, no integration with your point-of-sale system, no training course. The "enterprise AI" version, the one with data pipelines and consultants and months of setup, is a different product built for a different size of company. You're not shopping in that aisle.

## Why the fear feels bigger than the bill

The word "AI" gets used for two very different things, and small business owners usually only hear about one of them: the expensive, custom, months-long version built for companies with a hundred employees and a tech budget to match.

The version that actually helps a business your size is aimed at one annoying, repetitive task. Answering the same three customer questions. Writing the tenth version of a job posting. Drafting a follow-up to a lead who went quiet. That version doesn't need a budget meeting. It needs ten minutes and a task you're already tired of doing.

## The move: price one task, not "AI"

Stop asking "what does AI cost?" That question doesn't have a useful answer, same as asking "what does software cost." Ask a smaller, sharper question: what does it cost you, in time, to keep doing this one task by hand?

<Takeaway>

Pick one task this week. Something you or someone on your team does over and over: a follow-up email, a social post, a summary of customer reviews, an answer to a common question. Try it with a free AI tool for one week. Time how long it takes with the tool versus how long it usually takes without it.

If it saves real time, keep using it. It cost you nothing but ten minutes to find out. If it doesn't, you've lost nothing, and you've ruled out one guess.

Here's a copy-paste starting point if you want one. Open any free AI chat tool and paste this in, filling in your own task:

> I run a small business. Here is a task I do by hand every week: [describe the task]. Write me a first draft, or a short step-by-step process, to do this faster. Plain language, no jargon.

</Takeaway>

That's the whole experiment. No contract, no signup. Just one task, one week, and an honest look at whether it saved you time.
