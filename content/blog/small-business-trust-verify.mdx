A woman posts constantly about "supporting small businesses" and "shopping small." Beige packaging. Handwritten thank-you notes. The whole aesthetic. Then her friend calls her out in a Reddit post: she's buying from Temu and AliExpress, marking it up 300 percent, and slapping her own logo on a sticker. Same products. Same photos. Just a markup and a story.

The friend's reply, quoted in the post: she doesn't understand "curation" and "branding." Every small business starts somewhere. The friend who called her out is now the villain, accused of humiliating her, barely on speaking terms.

That post is real and live on r/AITApod right now. What happened next is the part worth paying attention to if you run an actual small business.

Reddit did not agree on a verdict. Some commenters sided with the friend who spoke up: reselling isn't a small business, it's a hustle wearing a costume. Others sided with the reseller: every retailer marks up goods they didn't make, that's just retail, tell that to Walmart. A few flagged the whole post as likely made up. Sixty-one comments in, nobody settled it.

That split is the actual story. If a crowd of strangers with no stake in the outcome can't agree on where curated resale ends and small business begins, your customers can't either. And they're not going to work it out with a debate. They're going to make a snap call based on whatever you show them.

Here's what that means for you, even if you've never touched a resold product: "small business" isn't a self-certifying label anymore. It's a claim people have started checking. Every story like this one, real, fake, or somewhere in between, trains customers to look twice at the next seller who uses the phrase. Including you.

You didn't do anything wrong. You still carry the cost. Customers don't audit sellers one at a time. They generalize from the last story they saw.

Waiting for the internet to settle what counts isn't a plan, because it clearly isn't going to settle anything. The move that actually protects you: make your version of "small business" something a customer can verify in ten seconds, not just something they have to take your word for.

That means a name. A location. A photo of where the work actually happens. A plain sentence about where the product comes from, not just a vibe. The reseller in the Reddit post had beige packaging and a handwritten note, real signals of effort, just attached to the wrong product. Effort on the wrapping doesn't answer the question customers are now asking. Proof does.

<Takeaway>

1. Add a name: your own, on your site and packaging, not just a brand name.
2. Add a location: your city, or a photo of where the work actually happens.
3. Add a real line about where the product comes from, not just a vibe.

</Takeaway>

This week, look at your own site, packaging, and bio the way that friend looked at the Temu boxes. If all you've got backing up "small business" is the phrase itself, add one detail a reseller couldn't fake: your name, your city, a photo of the actual workbench, a real line about where the product comes from.

The businesses that lose the most from stories like this aren't the ones people are arguing about on Reddit. They're the honest ones standing right next to them, saying nothing, assuming everyone already knows the difference.
