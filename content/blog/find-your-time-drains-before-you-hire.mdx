You're at your kid's soccer game, half watching, when your phone buzzes. It's the shop. A regular customer wants a rush order and nobody there can give them a yes or no without you. You step away from the sideline, make the call in ninety seconds, and walk back like nothing happened.

Then it happens again next Saturday. And the one after that.

By Monday you've decided you need to hire. More hands, fewer calls. That sounds right. Most weeks it isn't the first fix.

## Busy is not the same as short staffed

When you feel buried, a job post feels like the answer. But busy only tells you your hours are full. It doesn't tell you what's filling them.

A lot of owners never look. They feel the weight and go straight to payroll. That's a big fix for a problem nobody has measured yet.

And it isn't cheap. SHRM's 2025 benchmarking put the average cost per hire for a nonexecutive role at $5,475 ([SHRM, 2025](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-releases-2025-benchmarking-reports--how-does-your-organizat)). That's before the first paycheck.

## Where the hours actually go

A 2023 survey by Time Etc, a virtual assistant company, found that entrepreneurs running growing businesses work 45.5 hours a week on average. More than a third of that week, 36%, goes to admin tasks ([Startups Magazine, 2023](https://startupsmagazine.co.uk/article-how-entrepreneurs-may-be-unwittingly-keeping-their-businesses-small)). That works out to about 16 hours. Two full workdays of invoices, scheduling, data entry and chasing late payers.

An older survey points the same way. In a 2015 survey of 323 owners by The Alternative Board, owners worked 49.4 hours a week but felt they should work 41.7. Email and web browsing took 32% of their time ([The Alternative Board, 2015](https://www.thealternativeboard.com/time-management)).

Nobody started a business to answer email.

## The four drains to look for

Not every drain looks like one.

**Questions only you can answer.** The rush order call at the soccer game is one. Ask yourself: is this a capacity problem or a knowledge problem? If the rule lives only in your head, a new hire can't make the call either. They just text you, same as before. You're still the bottleneck. You just added payroll.

**The inbox.** You check it all day and answer things as they land. Each check feels small. Add them up and they eat a morning.

**Interruptions.** The "got a sec?" at your door. The call that could have waited until Tuesday. Each one costs more than the minute it takes, because you have to find your place again after.

**Admin you never meant to own.** The invoices you still send because you set them up in year one. The supply order. The schedule. You kept them because handing them off felt slower. In that Time Etc survey, 25% said they don't hand work off because by the time they'd explained it, it was faster to do it themselves. True the first time. Not the fiftieth.

## How to tell a drain from real work

Start by writing each one down for a week, the way the list at the bottom lays out. For each thing that pulls you away, ask one question. Could someone else do this within a month if they had simple written steps? If yes, it's a drain. Write the steps and hand it off, or stop doing it. If no, it truly needs you, and that's the work a hire should protect, not replace.

The rush order is a good example, because the rule is simple. Yes if it's a regular customer and the stock is there. No if it would bump another promised order. Written on one card by the register, your crew can make that call on day one. Left in your head, it buzzes in your pocket every Saturday.

You might be thinking you don't have time to track your time. Each line takes about ten seconds to jot down. If you can't spare ten seconds, that tells you something too.

## Then decide on the hire

Once the drains are cut or handed off, look at your week again. If you're still buried in real work that needs more hands, hire. Now you know what the job is, and you can write a job post that says more than "help me."

If the drains were most of it, you just skipped a hire, and a lot of Saturday phone calls.

<Takeaway title="One move to make this week">

1. For one week, keep a note on your phone. Every time something pulls you off what you meant to do, write it down: who asked, what it was, and about how long it took.
2. At the end of the week, sort each line. Could someone else do it with written steps, or does it truly need you?
3. Take the drain that showed up most. Write the rule or the steps on one page and hand it off, or stop doing it. Then look at that job post again.

</Takeaway>

The ninety seconds on the sideline were never the real cost. The drain nobody ever wrote down was.
