I had a client once who was completely sure her website looked great. It didn't. She saw polished and on-brand. Everyone else saw a ransom note built in WordArt. I couldn't talk her out of it, because she had never worked with a real designer long enough to see the gap between what she liked and what actually worked.

That gap is the real risk in small business marketing. It was there long before AI showed up. AI just makes it a lot faster to fill a website, or a stack of postcards, with it.

Right now there's a fight online about whether small business owners have any business using AI to design their own marketing. You can watch it play out on r/aiwars: https://www.reddit.com/r/aiwars/comments/1w6l3qs/outright_bashing_on_small_businesses_now/. One side calls it an insult to trained designers. The other side just wants their flyer done before lunch. Both sides are arguing about the wrong thing. The question was never whether a human or a machine made your postcard. It's whether the person running the prompt can tell good from bad.

Here's my actual take: most small business owners should be using AI for this, and most already are. A postcard, a social graphic, a flyer for a networking event, none of that takes the same skill as building a brand from nothing. It takes something that looks like you, on time, without a $2,000 invoice attached.

But the tool doesn't remove the need for an eye. It removes the need to pay someone else for one. A good prompt from someone with taste produces something usable. A bad prompt from someone without it produces something that technically has your logo on it and nothing else going for it.

The numbers show both halves of this playing out at once. Small business AI use jumped from 39 percent to 55 percent in a single year, according to Thryv's 2025 survey of small business owners, and most who use it now call it essential to reaching customers. Designers are adopting just as fast. A 2026 survey of over 900 designers from Designer Fund and Foundation Capital found weekly AI use for design work jumped from 54 percent to 91 percent in a year.

Here's the part that should worry you more than the debate does. Gartner surveyed U.S. consumers in late 2025 and found half of them would rather buy from a brand that skips generative AI in its marketing altogether. People don't hate the tool. They hate being able to tell. That's not a reason to stop using AI. It's a reason to stop using it lazily.

So here's what actually separates the businesses getting this right from the ones filling their feed with generic template graphics.

<Takeaway>

1. Pay for a real designer once, for your logo and your core website. You'll reuse both for years, and they set the bar everything else has to match.
2. After that, use AI for the repeatable stuff: postcards, social posts, one-off flyers. Tell it to match what you already have instead of generating its own default look, or you'll end up with the same flat AI style that's already starting to make people suspicious.
3. Before you publish anything you made yourself, show it to one person with real taste. Not your spouse. Not your co-founder, who thinks exactly like you do. Someone who will tell you straight if it looks like your business or looks like nothing at all.

</Takeaway>

If you're a designer reading this instead of an owner, the lesson runs the other way. Your value was never how fast you could push pixels around. It's that you already know what good looks like, which makes you faster and better with these tools than a client who's guessing. The owners embracing AI aren't your competition. The ones who never learn to see the difference are the problem, for both of you.

Pull up the last three things you designed yourself. Show them to someone with real taste and ask them straight: does this look like your business, or does it just look like something?
