This week, NBC News reported that $289 million Congress already approved for small business loans is being withheld, tied to an executive order that eliminated a long-standing small business fund. Everyone arguing about it online has strong opinions about why. Almost nobody is talking about the business owner who already made plans around that money.

Getting approved for an SBA loan feels like the finish line. It isn't. Approved means a bank and a federal agency agree you're a good bet. Available means the money is actually sitting somewhere you can spend it. Congress appropriating $289 million was supposed to close that gap. Instead the money is stuck between "approved" and "yours," and nobody's telling owners how long that gap stays open.

## I've done this four times. It's usually the boring kind of good.

Here's what a lot of the outrage misses. The SBA loan program itself is genuinely one of the better tools a small business owner has. I've helped clients get one four separate times over the years, and I used one myself when we renovated our first office and needed to buy equipment we couldn't cash-flow outright. If your financials are in order and the business is solid, it's not even that hard to get approved. It's a good program.

That's exactly why watching it get stuck like this is so frustrating. This isn't a shady backroom deal falling apart. It's the reliable option turning unreliable, for reasons that have nothing to do with your business.

## The clock was already slow. Now it's just a shrug.

Even when everything goes right, an SBA 7(a) loan takes 30 to 90 days from application to funding, averaging around two months. That's already a number you build a business decision around, not something you glance at once.

A frozen or indefinitely delayed disbursement doesn't add a few days to that number. It replaces the number with a shrug. You can plan around 60 days. You cannot plan around "we'll let you know."

## This has happened before. That's the actual problem.

This isn't a one-time glitch. During the 43-day government shutdown last fall, the SBA's own numbers showed it couldn't deliver $5.3 billion to 10,000 small businesses that were waiting on it. Separately, back in April, the Senate Small Business Committee flagged another $55 million being held back from Small Business Development Centers, Women's Business Centers, SCORE, and Veterans Business Outreach Centers, money those groups were owed for work they'd already done.

Different program. Different month. Same failure point: money Congress already set aside gets stuck somewhere upstream of the person who actually needed it. At some point that stops looking like an accident and starts looking like how the system just behaves whenever it hits friction.

## Bad form, no matter whose name is on the door

Here's my actual take, and it isn't about picking a side. Any administration that lets appropriated small business money sit frozen is doing something genuinely bad for the people it's supposed to help. That's true regardless of which party is in the building when it happens.

Most of the people who get to make that call have never run a business with 15 employees and a payroll due on Friday. A few have, a long time ago. It shows. Bureaucracy doesn't feel the deadline the way you do, because it doesn't have one.

## What to do with money you don't control

You can't fix the funding pipeline from your office. You can stop building your whole plan on top of it.

<Takeaway>

1. Don't sign a lease, a deposit, or an equipment order that assumes the money lands on a specific date, even after approval.
2. Build a real buffer, 30 days minimum, into anything tied to loan proceeds.
3. Call your lender now and ask directly what bridge options exist if disbursement stalls, before you need the answer instead of after, and check in with your loan officer yourself instead of waiting for a form email to tell you something's wrong.

</Takeaway>

None of that gets your money to you faster. It just means a stalled disbursement doesn't take your whole plan down with it.

## The real takeaway

You already measured for the building. You already told your equipment vendor a date. That's not naive, that's just what happens when a federal agency tells you your loan is approved. The plans are supposed to be safe to make.

This isn't about whether you support this administration or the last one or the next one. It's that owners keep building real plans on top of money they have zero control over, and every few months something upstream breaks it anyway. That's bad form, full stop, regardless of who signs the memo.

If you're waiting on SBA funds right now, call your loan officer this week and get a real answer, not a placeholder. Don't let silence be the update you're working off of.
